## Cole-Hopf Transformation
Given a non-linear equation of the form
$$
\begin{equation}
\partial_t J - a\partial_xJ - b(\partial_xJ)^2 + c \partial_{xx}J + \Phi(x,t) = 0, \tag{1}
\end{equation}
$$
the Cole-Hopf transformation provides for us an equivalent linear equation. The transformation is given by
$$
\begin{equation}
\psi(x,t)=\exp\left(-\lambda^{-1}J(x,t)\right), \tag{2a}
\end{equation}
$$
a bijection with inverse 
$$
\begin{equation}
J(x,t)=-\lambda\log(\psi(x,t)), \tag{2b}
\end{equation}
$$
where $\lambda$ is some constant whose value has yet to be determined.

## Green's functions of the heat equation (heat kernels)
### 1. Heat equation over the real line
$$
g(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2(t-t^\prime)}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(x-x^\prime)^2}{\sigma^2(t-t^\prime)}\right) \enspace &\text{for } t > t^\prime, \\
\delta(x-x^\prime) \enspace &\text{for } t = t^\prime,\\
0 \enspace &\text{for } t < t^\prime.
\end{cases}
$$
for all $x,x^\prime \in \mathbb{R}$. The heat kernel for the real line is a convolution kernel in the state variable $x$, which reflects the translational invariance of the heat equation on the real line. Restricting $t$ to the interval $t > t^\prime$, the heat kernel is again a convolution, reflecting invariance in positive time, but this does not hold over all $t$, as the heat kernel is uniformly zero for $t < t^\prime$. This reflects causality; source terms introduced at time $t^\prime>t$ are not "felt" at the earlier time $t$.

### 2. Heat equation over the half-line, Dirichlet boundary condition

The heat kernel for the right half-line $[a,\infty)$ is the same for $t \leq t^\prime$, and for $t > t^\prime$ we have
$$
g_{(a,\infty)}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) =
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2(t-t^\prime)}}
\left[
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{(x-x^\prime)^2}{t-t^\prime}\right) - \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{(x+x^\prime-2a)^2}{t-t^\prime}\right)
\right].
$$
This kernel can be obtained by summing two fundamental solutions of the heat equation in a way that one negates the other at the boundary $a$; this is the **method of images**. The kernel in this case is not a convolution kernel in state, as the presence of a boundary condition at a particular location $a$ has broken the translational invariance.

The heat kernel for the left half-line $(-\infty,b]$ is the same, but with opposite sign:
$$
g_{(-\infty,b)}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) =
\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2(t-t^\prime)}}
\left[
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{(x-x^\prime)^2}{t-t^\prime}\right) 
- \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{(x+x^\prime-2b)^2}{t-t^\prime}\right)
\right]
$$

### 3. Heat equation over the interval, Dirichlet boundary conditions

The heat kernel for an interval $[a,b]$ is obtained by restricting our consideration to fundamental solutions with periodicity $b-a$, and then enforcing the Dirichlet condition at point $a$ through the method of images using two such solutions (equivalently, at point $b$, as restricting ourselves to $(b-a)$-periodic functions has identified the two boundaries).

A $(b-a)$-periodic function can be expressed as an infinite sum of periodically-spaced solutions:
$$
\begin{align*}
\Theta_{b-a}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) 
&:= \sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}} g(x+n(b-a),t;x^\prime,t^\prime) \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2(t-t^{\prime})}}\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(x-x^\prime -n(b-a))^2}{\sigma^2(t-t^\prime)}\right).
\end{align*}
$$
Alternatively, we can use a Fourier series to find an equivalent expression by evaluating the Fourier transform of the fundamental solution on a discrete set of frequencies corresponding to the periodicity of the interval.


With the periodic function in hand, we can enforce the Dirichlet boundary conditions by subtracting the contributions from the images at the boundaries:
$$
\begin{align*}
g_{(a,b)}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) &= \Theta_{b-a}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) - \Theta_{b-a}(x-a,t;x^\prime-a,t^\prime) \\
&= \Theta_{b-a}(x,t;x^\prime,t^\prime) - \Theta_{b-a}(b-x,t;b-x^\prime,t^\prime).
\end{align*}
$$

## Example

Consider the case where the terminal cost is given by some centered quadratic, $C_T(x)=c_T x^2/2$, and the motion is governed by diffusion process $dx = udt + \sigma dB_t$ with a quadratic control cost of $C_u(u)=c_u u^2/2$.

$$
\psi(x,T) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{x^2}{\sigma^2c_u/c_T}\right).
$$

### 1. $\psi(x,t)$ over $(t_2,T]$
For all times up to $t_2$, the diffusion occurs over the entire real line, and thus is given by a convolution of $\psi_T$ and the heat kernel $g$. The result of the convolution will be a Gaussian centered at $0$, with variance $\lambda/c_T+\sigma^2t$ and integral equal to that of $\psi_T$:

$$\psi_T * g = \sqrt{\frac{c_u }{c_u + c_T(T-t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{c_Tx^2}{c_u+c_T(T-t)}\right).$$

This is an inverse transformation away from something familiar, a solution that we've already derived in not one, but two different ways in the previous blogpost!


### 2. $\psi(x,t)$ over $[t_1,t_2]$
At $t=t_2$, we have a new boundary condition, a homogeneous Dirichlet condition at $x=a$, and a restricted domain of $(a,\infty)$. We discard $\psi(x,t_2)$ for $x<a$ (it having been absorbed at the barrier) and convolve what remains with the heat kernel for the half-line, $g_{(a,\infty)}$:

$$
\begin{align*}
(\psi_{t_2}\mathbf{1}_{(a,\infty)}) * g_{(a,\infty)} &= \int_a^\infty g_{(a,\infty)}(x^\prime,t_2;x,t)\psi(x^\prime,t_2)dx^\prime \\
=\sqrt{\frac{c_u }{2\pi\sigma^2(t_2-t)(c_u + c_T(T-t_2))}}
\Biggl[
\int_a^\infty
&\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left(\frac{(x-x^\prime)^2}{t_2-t}+\frac{c_Tx^{\prime2}}{c_u+c_T(T-t_2)}\right)\right) dx^\prime\\
- \int_a^\infty&\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left(\frac{(x+x^\prime-2a)^2}{t_2-t}+
\frac{c_Tx^{\prime2}}{c_u+c_T(T-t_2)}\right)\right) dx^\prime
\Biggr] \\
=\sqrt{\frac{c_u(c_u+c_T(T-t))}{c_T(t_2-t)(c_u+c_T(T-t_2)}}
\Biggl[
&\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{c_Tx^2}{c_u + c_T(T-t)}\right)\int_a^\infty\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\
-&\exp
\end{align*}
$$
Defining
$$
\psi^0(x,t)=\exp( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\frac{c_T}{c_u + c_T(T-t)}x^2) \cdot \text{erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_u + c_T(T-t)}{2\sigma^2(t_2-t)(c_u + c_T(T-t_2))}}\left(a-\frac{c_u+c_T(T-t_2)}{c_u+c_T(T-t)}x\right)\right),
$$
we have that
$$
\psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_u}{c_u + c_T(T-t)}}\left[\psi^0(x,t) - \psi^0(2a-x,t)\right].
$$

### 3. $\psi(x,t)$ over $[0,t_1)$
Once we break past time $t_1$ we are once again in the unrestricted domain of the real line. At the instant $t=t_1$, $\psi$ is zero-valued for $x\leq a$, but it will spread out over the entire real line as time progresses under the action of the heat kernel, $g$.
